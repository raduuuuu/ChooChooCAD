Measuring a piece from photos

You need: the piece; one medium straight (144mm), which is the ruler in every photo; a sheet of white, blue or pink millimetre paper (shop-bought graph paper is fine, the brand and the exact print size do not matter; cream or yellow paper is as warm a colour as the wood, so the pieces can't be told from it); and a phone. For the first session only, also a long straight (216mm).

Sending your photos on GitHub

Photos you post on a GitHub issue are public, and GitHub keeps them online even if the issue is deleted later. Before you post one:

  • Remove where the photo was taken. Phones save the place in the photo. See the steps for your phone or computer in the contributing guide, or turn off location for the camera before you shoot.
  • Only the pieces and the paper in the frame: no people, faces, house numbers, letters or screens.

Why the medium straight: the tops of the pieces sit 12mm nearer the lens than the paper, so the paper's own lines would read them a few percent long. A known piece lying beside them, top face at the same height, gives the true scale. The paper's grid only straightens out the camera angle.

1 · Lay out the reference

  • Lay the paper flat on a table or a hard floor. Tape the corners if it curls.
  • Put the medium straight and the piece on it side by side, top face up, about 2cm apart, both fully on the grid.
  • Nothing under either piece. Both lie flat on the paper.

2 · Photograph from the top

  • Hold the phone flat, lens pointing straight down, over the middle of the two pieces, 30–40cm up.
  • Both pieces and a ring of grid around them in the picture. Use the normal lens (1x), never wide-angle (0.5x), never portrait mode.
  • Each piece crosses the middle of the picture: one end in the top half, the other in the bottom half. A piece lying wholly in one half shows the end face of its end nearer the middle, and reads long.
  • No shadow across an end. Hold still; a sharp photo matters more than a big one.

3 · A side or end photo, only if asked

You don't need to send one. If the top views leave a piece's shape unclear, for example a ramp or a station, the person drawing it will ask for a photo of its side or its end.

  • No paper and no ruler needed: this photo shows the shape only. Any height is read with a ruler instead, and you'll get a drawing of exactly where to measure.
  • Stand the piece on a table or the floor. Hold the phone upright and level with the piece, about 30–40cm away, facing the side (or the end) square on, with the whole piece in the picture.

4 · Move the piece and repeat

  • Lift both pieces and put them back somewhere else on the paper, still lying flat with the top face up. Turn them end for end if you like; never stand them on their sides.
  • Write down what is printed or moulded on the underside: versions of the same piece can differ. A photo of the underside only if someone asks for it.
  • Take the top photo again. Two photos must give the same sizes within 0.5mm; if they do not, take a third.
  • Save the photos upright. If you send them on GitHub, they become public (see the box at the top): remove where they were taken first. If you are drawing the piece yourself, they stay on your machine in the repo's photos/ folder, which is never uploaded.

5 · The first time: check with the long straight

Before any real piece is measured, photograph the long straight as if it were the piece, twice, steps 1, 2 and 4. It must read 216 ± 1mm. Until it does, no size from a photo goes into the library.

What a rejected photo looks like

  • The grid points disagree: the paper was not flat, two sheets showed, or a point was misread. Use one sheet; flatten or tape it.
  • The reference reads far off on the paper: it was not lying flat and top face up, or it is not a medium straight.
  • Curved grid lines: a wide-angle or portrait mode was on. Use the normal 1x lens.
  • A blurred or shadowed end: its point cannot be read. Retake it.
  • Two photos more than 0.5mm apart: take a third; the figure lands only when two agree.
